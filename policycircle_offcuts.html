The part you have already seen. For every page that matters, I design several directions and we pick between them, rather than me presenting one layout and asking for a yes.

Directions, not a draft

For the homepage you get several distinct directions: one that pushes well past the current site, one that stays close to it, and the ground in between.

You mix and match

Take the hero from one and the impact section from another. Showing options only works if the final page is assembled from what you actually respond to.

Then it gets built

Once a direction is agreed it goes into WordPress properly, with a written guide and a walkthrough recording so your team owns it rather than inherits it.

What I found

What I found when I looked at the site.

Everything here comes from the live site rather than from your brief. It's a surface read rather than the full audit that month one covers, but it already gives a good sense of where the ninety days should go.

01

There are three different ways of building a page

The main site runs Divi with a divi-child theme, the new Events subdomain is going into Elementor, and some pages are native WordPress. That means three template libraries and three sets of styles, so a change often has to be made more than once.

02

The old member portal is still generating pages

You mentioned roughly 4,000 pages sitting outside the index. Now that I've been through Search Console it's 4,619. A retired Higher Logic member portal published a page for every forum post on the main domain, old event pages on a dead template are still reachable, and the pre-2025 site was never fully removed.

03

GA4 and GTM are both running, and so is Site Kit

G-TYN0VYM5DG and GTM-NF859QGV both fire on the homepage, and Site Kit is installed alongside them. When there's more than one route into the same property, the numbers tend to drift apart over time.

04

I couldn't find a Meta Pixel

There's no connect.facebook.net anywhere on the homepage. If you're spending anything on Meta at the moment, there's no way to attribute it, retarget, or build audiences from what people do on the site.

05

The homepage is carrying a lot of weight

246KB of HTML before a single image loads, 20 stylesheet and script files, 34 script tags, and an embedded Instagram feed. That's fairly normal for a page builder, and it's probably the easiest thing on this list to improve.

06

A couple of your priority pages have their own issues

CLER repeats its course blocks word for word, and the $300 fee and the time commitment sit around 80% down the page. Who We Are runs to more than 70 people across founders, board, staff, coaches and a 40+ advisory council, so splitting it makes a lot of sense.

None of this is unusual for a site that has grown as much as yours has, and all of it is fixable.

What's not included

What isn't part of this phase.

A ninety-day phase works because of what it leaves out, so it's worth naming these now rather than later.

  • A rebrand. This works inside your existing brand: the navy, the terracotta and the existing mark. Type and hierarchy improve, but the identity itself doesn't change.
  • A re-platform. You'd stay on WordPress and WP Engine. No CMS migration, no headless rebuild, no new hosting.
  • A full site rebuild. Six to nine pages get rebuilt. The rest of the site carries on working and picks up the global header, footer and type scale.
  • A membership or CRM migration. The Membership page sunsets and its URLs are handled, but moving member records between systems would be separate work.
  • Content writing at volume. Editing, restructuring and cutting your existing copy is included. Writing new long-form content isn't.
  • Paid media management. The tracking gets set up so campaigns can be measured, but running them isn't part of this phase.
  • Routine WordPress maintenance. Core, theme and plugin updates stay with the team already doing them. I'm not proposing to take that over or charge you for it.
  • The audience journey and messaging strategy. That work is yours and your strategy partner's. This phase builds and measures what comes out of it.

Anything here can move into scope if you'd like it to. Each has a price and most have a fairly obvious Phase 2 shape. It just seemed more useful to name them now than to run into them in week ten.

Findings that came out

Still true, just not on the page.

01

The index is shrinking

Indexed pages have gone down in almost every reading since May, from 1,670 to 1,084. That's a fall of about a third in under three months.

02

The whack-a-mole, with a number on it

180 pages with redirects, 90 soft 404s, 89 genuine 404s and 2 redirect errors. That's the pattern you described on our call, and it's around 360 URLs rather than the handful it feels like when you stumble across them one at a time.

03

A minor one: robots.txt is blocking all your uploads

The whole file is a single rule, Disallow: /wp-content/uploads/, and that's what the 1,086 "blocked by robots.txt" pages are. It keeps your images out of Google Images, and the 132 listed as indexed despite being blocked are files Google knows about but can't read. Not urgent, but worth a decision rather than leaving it as it is.

04

A quarter of your impressions aren't really impressions

233 of your top pages are table-of-contents anchor links, things like /briefs/the-executive-branch/#toc-introduction. Between them they have 13.4m impressions and 291 clicks. That's 27% of everything, at a click-through rate of 0.002%, and it quietly drags down every average anyone looks at.

Kept in a shorter form

The anchor-link finding survives as a Technical item ("Search Console & the anchor links"). The 180 / 90 / 89 numbers survive inside the legacy page clearance item. The shrinking index and the robots.txt point are gone from the client document entirely.

01

If one in a hundred joined the newsletter

That would be 67 women a month, or around 800 a year. Against a community of 15,000 that's growth of roughly 5%, from a change that costs nothing to run once it's built. One in a hundred is a modest rate for a real ask on a page someone is already reading by choice.

02

If one in two hundred went on to CLER

That would be 33 a month. CLER and the programs currently get about 40 a month from search in total, so it would come close to doubling them, without spending anything on advertising and without writing a single new brief.

03

Why that's a range and not a promise

I don't know yet which of those rates is real for your audience, and honestly nobody does until it's measured. What I can do is put the tracking in place in month one, so that by the end of the ninety days you have the actual number rather than my estimate, and you know whether this is worth building on in Phase 2.

It's also why I'd add the newsletter path to your list of pages. On these numbers it's the cheapest thing in the whole phase and quite possibly the most valuable.
